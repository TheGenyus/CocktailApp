Avis:
Bilan : Tous les barmans péruviens que j'ai rencontrés préfèrent leurs Pisco Sours mélangés plutôt que secoués.En effet, c'est un cocktail génial une fois mélangé, mais je préfère mon Pisco Sours secoué.(À Cuba, les Daiquiris ont également tendance à être mélangés, mais je préfère aussi ceux secoués.) Alors que certains aiment omettre le blanc d'œuf, comme la plupart des cocktails aigre-doux, le Pisco Sour est meilleur avec l'œuf, bien que la moitié du blanc d'un petit blanc d'œuf frais soit suffisant (environ 15 ml/½ oz à 20 ml/⅔).Certains préconisent également l'utilisation de sucre en poudre à la place du sirop de sucre en poudre, et en effet, cela peut ajouter à cette acidité (comme le Daiquiri).Dans ce cas, vous aurez besoin de 3 cuillères (un bol doseur de 5 ml) de sucre en poudre à la place du sirop.Le choix du pisco mérite davantage de débat.Mes amis péruviens privilégient le pisco à base de cépage Quebranta ou même un acholado (un mélange de variétés).Cependant, je préfère utiliser du pisco péruvien fabriqué à partir du cépage Italia plus aromatique, de préférence le pisco mosto verde Italia.Alternativement, il existe également des alternatives au pisco chilien d’un bon rapport qualité-prix.Il est convenu que le Pisco Sour est garni de trois gouttes d'amers aromatiques déposées symétriquement sur sa tête moussante.Cela ajoute non seulement à l’apparence visuelle, mais l’arôme du bitter aide également à masquer l’odeur de chien mouillé du blanc d’œuf.L'Angostura ou des bitters aromatiques similaires conviennent, mais pour plus d'authenticité, pensez à utiliser des bitters péruviens Amargo Chuncho, qui sont à base de diverses écorces et herbes amazoniennes.Certains saupoudrent également la surface du cocktail ou le bord du verre de cannelle.De manière quelque peu controversée, si l'utilisation du pisco Italia ne suffit pas, j'aime aussi ajouter un trait d'eau de fleur d'oranger à mon Pisco Sours.J'ai également tendance à les servir directement dans un verre à l'ancienne congelé à fond épais pour prolonger la durée pendant laquelle le cocktail reste glacé, mais une coupe glacée fonctionne également bien (et est plus belle sur les photos).Enfin, j'avais l'habitude de spécifier uniquement le jus de citron vert, mais je privilégie désormais une répartition riche en citron vert entre le jus de citron vert et le jus de citron.Cela dit, 22,5 ml (¾oz) de jus de citron vert donnent un cocktail très savoureux et plus simple.

Histoire:
Like so many vintage cocktails, the documented origins of the Pisco Sour have changed over recent years as evidence of earlier origins has been discovered. Back in the mid-1980s, a Chilean newspaper, El Comercio de Iquique , reported that the pisco sour was created in 1872 by Elliot Stubb, an English steward from a sailing ship named Sunshine, who opened a bar in the then Peruvian port of Iquique (now in northern Chile). However, closer scrutiny shows a quote attributed to Stubb was actually about the Whiskey Sour , the forerunner to the Pisco Sour, the origins of which lie even earlier in the United States. In 2009, Luis Guillermo Toro-Lira Stahl and Michael P. Morris published a document Clarifying the legends in the history of pisco sour . This traced the origins of the Pisco Sour to Victor Vaughen Morris Jones (Victor Morris), the grandfather of one of the authors. Victor Morris originated from Salt Lake City, Utah, but immigrated to Peru in 1903, initially working as a cashier for the Cerro de Pasco Railroad railway company in the town of Cerro de Pasco. Legend, and indeed members of his family, say that while working here at the inauguration of the line from Oroyo, Morris created the Pisco Sour after running out of whiskey for the Whiskey Sours he was making for the reported 5,000 people in attendance. Morris moved to Lima where, backed by long-term friend Daniel C. Babbitt, on 1st April 1916, he opened the appropriately named Morris' Bar at 847 Calle Boza. Here, Morris, who was also known by his nickname Gringo, became noted for his Pisco Sours and the bar prospered. The bar's original 82-page visitor books, titled Morris' Bar Register , still in the hands of the Morris family, record more than 2,200 signatures, their names showing that it was a high-end establishment attracting many visiting Americans, including diplomats, businessmen, lawyers, and journalists. Notable names in the journal include Emiliano Figueroa, former president of Chile and ambassador to Peru; millionaire Roger W. Straus and Elmer Faucett, founder of the Company of Faucett Aviation. Perhaps most interesting is John Lannes from the Bank Exchange in San Francisco, famous for the Pisco Punch . Numerous remarks from visitors in this register testify to the delicious nature of Morris' Pisco Sours. As does a July 1924 notice for the Morris' Bar in the South Pacific Mail , a newspaper from the Chilean port of Valparaiso published weekly in English by Nelson Rounsevell, a friend of Morris who had also worked in Cerro de Pasco. The advertisement asks, "Have you registered at the Morris Bar LIMA?" It goes on to say the register at the bar can be helpful to contact [English-speaking] acquaintances, and the advert ends with "has been known for many years for their 'Pisco Sours'." An earlier mention of the Pisco Sour in the 22nd April 1921 edition of the Peruvian magazine Mundial describes the drink as a white-coloured beverage and attributes its invention to "Mister Morris". The 1929 Lima directory - The City of the Viceroys , lists the Pisco Sour as one of Morris Bar's cocktails under the heading "visitelo y pida alguna especialidad" (visit it and ask some speciality). Sadly for Victor, it appears that bartenders working at Morris' Bar left, taking his recipe with them to nearby establishments with bars such as the Hotel Bolivar (opened December 1924) and the Lima Country Club (opened February 1927). The competition for wealthy foreign guests obviously had a detrimental effect on his bar's success. Victor's fortunes and his health declined, eventually leading to his declaring voluntary bankruptcy and the closure of Morris Bar in 1929. He died a few months later, on 11th June, of cirrhosis, aged 56. In December, Morris' wife, Maria Vargas, emigrated to San Francisco, California, with their three children. With its large turnover of guests, the bar at The Hotel Bolivar, which was largely staffed by ex-Morris bartenders, is widely credited for spreading the popularity of Pisco Sour. The drink's survival is also attributed to Mario Alfonso Bruijet Burgos, a bartender who worked at Morris' Bar from 1924 and continued serving Pisco Sours at the Hotel Maury until his retirement. Some say that it is Bruijet who added bitters to the recipe after his move to the Maury. With a few twists, turns and variations, the above was the accepted origin of the Pisco Sour. That was until 22nd February 2012, when Peruvian writer Raúl Rivera Escobar uploaded a scan of a pamphlet published in Lima in 1903 by S.E. Ledesma . Titled Nuevo Manual de Cocina a la Criolla (New Manual of Creole Cooking), on page 32, among the food recipes, this includes a recipe simply titled 'Cocktail', containing " egg white, a cup of pisco, a teaspoon of sugar and a few drops of lime juice to taste ." Obviously a Pisco Sour, this is the earliest known recipe.
An egg, a glass of pisco, a teaspoon of fine sugar and a squeeze of lime, will open up a good appetite. You can make up to three cups with an egg and a well-rounded teaspoon of fine sugar, adding otherwise much more per cup. Hit [shake] all this in a shaker or punch bowl, to form a little punch. Nuevo Manual de Cocina a la Criolla - translation of the "Coctail" paragraph on page 32, 1903
So the Pisco Sour dates from 1903 or earlier and looks to be of Peruvian rather than Chilean origin. We shall probably never know who was first to take a Whiskey Sour recipe with pisco as the base spirit. Or indeed who was first to add bitters to it. That said, it's a wonderful coincidence that Victor Morris also emigrated to Peru in 1903! The fact that the Pisco Sour has been well-known outside of its native Peru and Chile for decades is attributed to Joe Baum, who promoted the drink in the 1960s at La Fonda Del Sol in New York, USA. Indeed, the popularity of Pisco Sour continues to grow, and International Pisco Sour Day is celebrated around the world on the first Saturday of February every year.
